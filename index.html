Restaurants in the Bronx and Queens are less likely to recover an A grade

On 3,921 paired NYC DOHMH initial → re-inspection visits since 2016, Bronx and Queens restaurants have significantly worse odds of landing a B or C grade at re-inspection than Manhattan restaurants — holding prior inspection score, cuisine, and re-inspection year fixed. Brooklyn and Staten Island aren't statistically distinguishable from Manhattan.

The finding

A borough-specific gap, not an outer-borough effect

A restaurant-clustered logistic regression on 3,921 linked initial → re-inspection visits (3,462 unique restaurants) finds that Bronx (OR 1.36, 95% CI 1.07–1.74, p=.014) and Queens (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.06–1.52, p=.009) restaurants have significantly worse odds of a B/C grade at re-inspection than Manhattan, adjusted for prior score, cuisine, and year. Brooklyn (OR 1.16, p=.119) and Staten Island (OR 0.78, p=.207) are not statistically distinguishable from Manhattan — so this isn't "outer boroughs vs. Manhattan," it's specific to the Bronx and Queens.

Prior inspection score matters too: every 10-point increase in the initial score corresponds to 1.22× higher odds of a B/C grade at re-inspection (95% CI 1.16–1.28, p<.001) — worse initial citations predict worse re-inspection outcomes, as expected, and the borough effect holds after controlling for it.

Why does the gap exist?

Six mechanisms tested across two rounds

Round 1 (investigate_borough_gap.py) tests three candidate explanations against the full 50,000-row local extract. None of them account for the gap.

✕ Ruled out

Is the initial citation itself worse in the Bronx/Queens?

No, not meaningfully. Violation counts, critical-violation counts, and inspection scores at the initial visit are nearly identical across boroughs (1.29 violations/visit in Manhattan vs. 1.31–1.34 elsewhere).

✕ Ruled out

Do Bronx/Queens restaurants have less time to fix the problem?

Ruled out — in the wrong direction, if anything. Bronx and Queens restaurants are re-inspected sooner (median 124–127 days) than Manhattan (146 days), and faster re-inspection correlates with a slightly lower B/C rate, not a higher one (r=0.037).

✕ Ruled out

Are they failing to fix the exact same violation?

Partially true (Bronx recurrence: 16.8% vs. Manhattan's 13.8%), but doesn't explain the overall gap — within each borough, recurrence barely moves the B/C rate. The gap shows up broadly, including at restaurants with entirely new violations at re-inspection.

◐ Suggestive, not conclusive

Does restaurant density explain the gap?

Adding a density proxy (restaurants per community board) shrinks both borough effects below significance (Bronx p=.018→.358, Queens p=.007→.076) — the strongest lead so far. But density is itself correlated with being in Manhattan, so this could be a real confound or just collinearity inflating the uncertainty.

? Untestable with this data

Does reporting lag explain the gap?

Not ruled out — genuinely unmeasurable here. The field that looked like a publish date (record_date) is actually a data-download timestamp (3 distinct values across 50,000 rows); grade_date equals inspection_date on every graded row.

? Untestable with this data

Does inspector assignment explain the gap?

Still untestable — all 31 fields in the extract were enumerated by hand; none identify an inspector, team, or staffing level. DOHMH doesn't publish this.

Five of six candidate mechanisms are now addressed: three ruled out, one suggestive-but-ambiguous (density), two confirmed untestable with public data. Restaurant density is the strongest lead this analysis has produced, but it stops at "worth a cleaner test," not "explains the gap." Full write-up in INVESTIGATION.md.

The data

NYC DOHMH Restaurant Inspection Results

Source: NYC DOHMH Restaurant Inspection Results (295,054 rows). One row = one violation cited on one inspection — not one restaurant, not even one visit. A restaurant cited for six things on one visit produces six rows with the same camis, so any real analysis has to group by restaurant and inspection date first, or it double-counts. See NOTES.md for the full list of data-quality gotchas (borough capitalization silently zeroing out API queries, dba name variants, missing-not-null violation fields).

Modeling choices worth flagging

  • Pre-2023 re-inspections are collapsed into a single "2022 or earlier" category instead of one dummy per year — only 29 eligible observations fall before 2023, too few to estimate stable year-by-year coefficients. Year is a control here, not the variable of interest, so this doesn't affect the borough estimates directly.
  • Cuisine categories with fewer than 20 observations are folded into "Other / rare cuisine" to avoid unstable single-cell estimates.

Limitations

  • Single-pull sample, not the full 295k-row table — a fuller extract would let the year-bucketing above be un-collapsed.
  • Cross-sectional, not causal: a borough's worse odds could reflect inspector assignment, restaurant density, reporting lag, or unmeasured neighborhood factors, not something intrinsic to the borough itself.
  • Cuisine folding trades cuisine-level detail for model stability, the same way the year-bucketing does.

Reproduce this

Run the analysis locally

Requires Python 3 with pandas, numpy, and requests (for the downloader).

pip install -r requirements.txt
python3 analyze_reinspection_model.py               # borough-adjusted logistic model
python3 investigate_borough_gap.py                  # round 1 of the mechanism hunt
python3 investigate_deeper_mechanisms.py             # round 2 of the mechanism hunt
python3 data/restaurant-analysis/download_data.py    # regenerate the 50k-row extract
python3 -m unittest discover -s tests -v             # run the test suite
